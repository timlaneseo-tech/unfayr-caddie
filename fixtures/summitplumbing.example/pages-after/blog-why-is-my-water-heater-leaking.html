Why Is My Water Heater Leaking?

By the Summit Plumbing team. Updated June 2025.

If you have lived through a Boise winter, you know how much we lean on our water heaters. Between the frosty mornings and the long showers that follow them, the tank in your garage or utility closet works harder from November through March than it does the rest of the year combined. So it is no surprise that this is also the season we get the most calls that start with the words "I think my water heater is leaking."

A leak is never something to ignore, but it is also not always the disaster it looks like. In this post we will walk through what we check when we arrive at a home with water on the floor around the heater, the most common causes we find, and how to decide whether you are looking at a quick fix or a replacement.

First, Make Sure It Is Actually the Water Heater

Before you panic, confirm that the water is coming from the heater and not from somewhere nearby. Water softeners, furnace condensate lines, and a nearby washing machine supply hose are all regular culprits that happen to sit next to the water heater. Wipe the area dry, lay down a paper towel, and check back in an hour or two. If the towel stays dry, the heater is probably not your problem.

Also remember that in a cold garage, a gas water heater can produce condensation on the outside of the tank when it fires up after a long idle period, especially if it is undersized for the household. That condensation drips down and looks exactly like a leak, but it stops once the tank is up to temperature.

Common Causes We See

Once you have confirmed the heater is the source, the location of the water tells most of the story. Here are the causes we run into most often across the Treasure Valley.

Loose Connections at the Top

The cold water inlet and hot water outlet sit on top of the tank, and the fittings there can loosen with years of expansion and contraction. Water appears on top of the tank and runs down the sides. This is usually the cheapest fix on this list: a technician tightens or replaces the fitting, and in many cases you can do it yourself with a wrench if the fittings are accessible.

The Temperature and Pressure Relief Valve

The T&P valve is a safety device on the side or top of the tank with a discharge pipe running toward the floor. It opens if the tank gets too hot or the pressure climbs too high. A valve that drips occasionally may just be doing its job, which points to a pressure problem in your home's plumbing. A valve that drips constantly has usually failed and needs to be replaced, which is an inexpensive part and about an hour of labor.

The Drain Valve

Near the bottom of the tank there is a drain valve, usually plastic on builder-grade heaters, used to flush sediment. These valves get brittle, and if one has been opened for a flush and not fully closed, it will weep. A leak here is a small repair unless the valve has cracked, in which case the tank needs to be drained to replace it.

Why is my water heater leaking from the bottom?

A leak from the bottom of the tank that is not coming from the drain valve means the tank has corroded through, and a corroded tank cannot be repaired. Shut off the cold water supply and the gas or power, then call a plumber the same day, because a failed tank can release its full forty or fifty gallons without warning. If the water is only at the drain valve, that valve can be replaced on its own.

Sediment buildup is the quiet cause behind a lot of water heater problems in Idaho. Our water is moderately hard, and over the years minerals settle to the bottom of the tank and bake onto the steel. That layer makes the burner work harder and overheats the bottom of the tank, which is what eventually causes the steel to crack.

When a tank leaks from the bottom, and the drain valve is not the source, the tank itself has almost always corroded through. There is no repair for a cracked tank. Replacement is the only option, and because a corroded tank can fail suddenly and release the entire forty or fifty gallons, we treat these calls as urgent. If you see rust-colored water pooling at the base of the heater, or a steady leak you cannot trace to a valve, shut off the water supply and the gas or power and call a plumber the same day.

Internal tank leaks can also show up as water leaking from the bottom element cover on an electric heater. The gasket around the element can fail, and water escapes behind the access panel. That one is a repair, not a replacement, as long as the tank itself is sound.

How Old Is Your Water Heater?

Age matters when you are deciding between repair and replacement. A standard tank water heater lasts about ten to twelve years in our area. If yours is approaching that age and leaking from the tank, repairing it rarely makes sense. If it is under eight years old and leaking from a fitting or valve, a repair is almost always the right call. You can find the manufacture date encoded in the serial number on the label; our post on how long water heaters last explains how to read it.

What We Recommend

Turn off the cold water supply valve above the heater. If it is a gas unit, turn the gas control to "off" or "vacation." If it is electric, switch off the breaker. Then call us. We will diagnose the leak, give you a flat price before any work starts, and if the tank needs replacing we can usually have a new one installed the same day.
